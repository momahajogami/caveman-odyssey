Sparta

Book IV, Part 1 — Telemakos reaches palace of Menelaos. Helene puts something in wine.

ETEONEOS

Menelaos. Two strangers at gate. Young men. Look important. Do we take horses? Or send them somewhere else?

MENELAOS

Eteoneos. You say stupid thing. We have eaten food of many strangers on long road home from Troy. We did not know their names. Zeus sends strangers. Take horses. Bring men inside.

Gold everywhere. Silver. Ivory. Telemakos and Peisistratos stare.
TELEMAKOS — whispering to Peisistratos

Look at this. Look at all of this. This is what inside of Zeus palace must look like. I have never seen anything like this. I cannot even speak.

MENELAOS

Yes. Much gold. Much silver. Much of everything. I went very far to get it. Eight years I wandered — Kypros, Phoinikia, Egypt, Libya, far Aithiopians — before I came home. And while I was gone, someone killed my brother Agamemnon. So I have all this gold. And I would give all of it, every piece, to have back three men who died. Most of all one man who suffered most, whose fate I cannot even speak of without grief — Odysseus. Somewhere his old father suffers. Somewhere his wife cries. Somewhere his son grows up without him.

Telemakos pulls cloak over eyes. Menelaos sees. Says nothing yet.
HELENE

Menelaos. Who are these young men? I have never seen anyone look so much like one person in my life. This boy — this is son of Odysseus. This is Telemakos. He was baby when his father went to Troy for my sake. For my sake that man left his family. I have never stopped feeling that.

MENELAOS

Yes. Same hands. Same eyes. Same way he held his head. I was just speaking of his father.

PEISISTRATOS

Menelaos, son of Atreus. Yes — this is Telemakos, son of Odysseus. He comes to ask if you have news of father. I am Peisistratos, son of Nestor. Nestor sent me with him. My own brother died at Troy — Antilochos. I never knew him. He died before I was old enough to know him. But people say he was fastest runner and best fighter of all men there. I carry that.

MENELAOS

Antilochos. Yes. I knew him. He was everything they say. And now you, his brother, come to my house. You are both welcome here as sons. Eat. Tomorrow we will talk about fathers. Tonight: eat and rest. Grief can wait one night.

Helene puts something in wine — drug from Egypt, gift from Egyptian queen. Takes away grief. Takes away anger. Man whose father and mother just died, if he drinks this — he will not cry that night.
HELENE

Let me tell you something about Odysseus. I saw him once in Troy — he had disguised himself as beggar, covered in wounds, rags, filth. He walked right into city to spy. Everyone else was fooled. I knew him. I took him inside. He told me everything — Greek plans, where ships were, what was going to happen. I kept his secret. I wanted to go home by then. I was tired of Troy. I was tired of all of it. He trusted me and I kept faith with him. When he left he killed many Trojans. That was Odysseus — walking into enemy city alone, in disguise, and walking out.

MENELAOS

Yes. And there is another story. End of war. We were inside wooden horse — all hidden, waiting. You came and walked around outside, calling names of Greek heroes in voices of their wives. I do not know how you knew their wives' voices. Some of us wanted to answer. Some of us almost cried out. Odysseus held us. He put his hand over mouths. He kept us still. One man — Antiklos — almost answered your call. Odysseus held his mouth shut with both hands until you walked away. That is how we survived to take Troy. That was Odysseus.

They sit with these stories a long time. Then they sleep.
Homer Very Small — after Homer — Ralph Hutchison
