The chariot is ready at the gate. Two horses, both good, both rested — Nestor does not send guests away on bad horses.

Peisistratos takes the reins. He is easy company — he asks questions without prying, answers questions without performing. His brother Antilochos died at Troy before Peisistratos was old enough to remember him, and there is something in the way he travels with Telemakhos — two young men in a chariot looking for news of fathers and brothers — that neither of them mentions but both of them feel.

They ride. The road from Pylos climbs inland and then runs south through the flat land toward Lakedaimon. The sun is warm. The horses settle into a good pace. The wheels raise a little dust.

Nestor is behind them somewhere, still at his gate, watching them go. He does not call after them. There is nothing more to say.

By evening they reach the house of Diokles — a man who knows Nestor, knows the road, takes travelers in. They eat and sleep there, and in the morning they go on.

Ahead of them somewhere: Sparta, and Menelaos, and whatever he knows.
