Mentor stands up.

He is an old man — Odysseus left him in charge of the household when he sailed for Troy, trusted him above everyone. He has watched the suitors for three years. He does not address them. He turns to the men of Ithaka.

"I want to say something to all of you," he says. "Not to the suitors — they are doing what they think is in their interest, and they will answer for it someday. I want to speak to the rest of you. You are sitting here watching a good king's son ask for help, and you are doing nothing. That is the thing that troubles me. Odysseus was fair to all of you — when did he ever act against anyone in this island? And now his son stands up alone and asks, and you are silent."

Leokritos — a suitor, and a loud one — stands up before Mentor has finished. "Mentor," he says, "sit down. Even if Odysseus came home this minute, do you think he could drive us out? There are too many of us. The woman needs to choose. Telemakhos needs to accept it." He looks around the assembly. "Go home, all of you. Let Mentor and Halitherses here fit out the ship if they think there's one to be had."

He dismisses the assembly. People get up and begin to leave. The suitors drift back toward the palace, talking among themselves. The elders go their separate ways.

Telemakhos walks down to the water. He washes his hands in the sea — a small, private gesture, a prayer — and stands looking out.

Behind him the town goes about its day. He is alone on the shore.
