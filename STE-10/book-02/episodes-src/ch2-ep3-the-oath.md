Eurykleia takes his hands.

"Child," she says — she still calls him that, and always will. "Why are you doing this? Your father is already gone. You are the only one left. If you sail away from here and something happens —" She stops. "Your mother will grieve herself to death. She is holding on by a thread as it is. You are the thread."

"I know," Telemakhos says. He doesn't pull his hands away.

"Then don't go."

"The gods have sent me," he says. "Athena has sent me." He says it simply, without drama — it is not a figure of speech, it is what he believes. "I have to go to Pylos and then to Sparta to ask about my father. I have to find out if he is alive or dead. I cannot stay here not knowing."

Eurykleia is crying. She doesn't try to stop.

"I need you to promise me something," he says. "Don't tell my mother. Not for twelve days — or until she asks where I am and someone tells her I'm gone. I don't want her grieving those twelve days when she doesn't need to. Can you promise me that?"

She looks at him for a long moment. Then she wipes her face.

"Give me your hand," she says.

He gives her his hand and she holds it and swears by Zeus and by Odysseus that she will keep silent. She goes back to fill the jars. He stands alone in the storeroom for a moment in the dark and the smell of beeswax, and then he goes to wait for nightfall.
