Penelope goes to her room and does not eat.

She prays — not formally, not with ritual, just talking into the dark the way people talk when there is no one else to talk to. She asks Athena. She asks anyone who is listening. She is more afraid for Telemakhos than she has let herself be for Odysseus, because Telemakhos is sixteen and she knows exactly where the danger is, whereas Odysseus and his absence have become a long grief with no specific shape.

She falls asleep on the floor, still in her day clothes.

Athena sends a figure — a phantom made to look like Penelope's sister Iphthime. It comes through the door like water under a door and stands at her head.

"Penelope," it says. "Don't be so afraid. He is not sailing unguarded. Athena is with him."

Penelope is half-asleep and half-awake, the way you are when something finds you in that threshold. She knows and does not know that this is a dream. "But what about Odysseus?" she asks. "Is he alive? Will he come home?"

The phantom is quiet for a moment. "I cannot tell you that," it says. "I was not sent to tell you that."

And then it is gone, back through the door the way it came.

Penelope wakes in the gray before dawn, alone on the floor of her room. The suitors' fires have gone out in the hall below. In the strait between Ithaka and Samos, a ship is waiting in the dark.

Her son is somewhere on the water.
