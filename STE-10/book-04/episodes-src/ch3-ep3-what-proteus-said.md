Proteus told Menelaos many things that day on the beach.

He told him about Ajax, who drowned in a shipwreck off the Gyraean rocks — drowned raging, still shouting that the gods hadn't beaten him, until Poseidon cracked the rock he was clinging to and pushed him under. He told him about Agamemnon — that he had come home and been killed, that this was already done, that Menelaos would have to live with that knowledge. He told Menelaos what he had done wrong on his own voyage and how to correct it.

And then he told him about Odysseus.

"He is alive," Proteus said. "He is on an island — the island of Kalypso, daughter of Atlas, far out in the sea. She keeps him there. He has no ship, no crew. He sits on the shore and looks at the water and weeps." He paused. "He wants very much to go home. That is all I know."

Menelaos finishes the story and looks at Telemakhos across the table. The morning light is in the room now, clear and direct.

Telemakhos is still. He is thinking about a man sitting on a shore, looking at the water, wanting to go home. He has been told this before. It does not get easier to hear. If anything it gets harder, because now he can see the shore more clearly — an island at the edges of the world, the man alone, the water going on forever in every direction.

"Thank you," Telemakhos says. It is insufficient. He says it anyway.
