"Toward the end of the war," Helen says, "Odysseus disguised himself as a beggar."

He did it properly — covered himself in filth, cuts on his own skin, rags, the whole performance. He walked into Troy through the gate in the middle of the day. The Trojans who saw him saw a filthy beggar. He walked through the streets and into the city he had been besieging for nine years and no one stopped him.

I was the only one who knew him.

She says it without pride. It is just what happened. "I had an eye for him — I'd watched him from the walls often enough. I knew his walk. I took him inside, I cleaned him up, I gave him food. He told me everything: where the ships were, what the Greeks were planning, how long until the end. I kept his secret. I wanted to go home by then. I had wanted to go home for a long time." She stops. "He killed a number of Trojans on his way out. That was also him — to walk into an enemy city alone, in disguise, gather everything he needed, and walk out."

She is quiet for a moment. The drug is working on all of them — the story comes in clearly, without the usual accompaniment of grief.

Telemakhos is watching her face. He is learning his father from the outside in, piece by piece, in rooms his father once walked through.
