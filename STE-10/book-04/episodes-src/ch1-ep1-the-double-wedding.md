They arrive at Sparta in the middle of a feast.

Menelaos is marrying off two children at once — his son Megapenthes to a girl from a good family, and his daughter Hermione (Helen's daughter, the one born before the war) to Achilles' son Neoptolemos. The hall is full and loud, musicians playing, the smell of roasting meat drifting out to the road.

Telemakhos and Peisistratos stop their horses at the gate. A servant named Eteoneos comes out and looks at them — two young men, well-dressed, clearly from somewhere, clearly not expected.

He goes back inside and finds Menelaos. "Two strangers at the gate," he says. "They look like they might be sons of kings. Should I have their horses stabled? Or send them somewhere else?"

Menelaos stares at him. "Eteoneos," he says, with the particular patience of a man who has been traveling for twenty years and knows what it means to be a stranger at a gate. "We have eaten at many tables we had no claim on. We did not know their owners' names. Zeus sends strangers. Stable the horses. Bring them in."

Eteoneos goes back out and leads Telemakhos and Peisistratos inside.
