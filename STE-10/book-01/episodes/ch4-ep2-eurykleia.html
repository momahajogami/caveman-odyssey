Chapter 4 · Episode 4.2

Eurykleia

Book I, lines 428–444 — The nurse takes Telemakhos to bed. The first day ends.

The suitors are dancing. Someone has found a faster song.

Telemakhos goes to bed.

Eurykleia carries the torch. She is old — very old — and she has been in this house longer than anyone alive remembers. Laertes, Odysseus's father, bought her when she was young, paid a great price for her, and treated her with honor all her life. She raised Odysseus from a baby. And then, in what she probably thought would be her quiet years, she raised Telemakhos.

She lights his room with the torch. She helps him take off his belt, his tunic. She folds the tunic carefully and hangs it on a wooden peg. She moves around the room the way she has moved around rooms her whole life — without wasted steps, doing the next thing that needs doing. She knows where everything is in the dark.

At the door she pauses. She has been watching him all evening from across the room. "You're thinking," she says. It is not a question.

"Go to sleep, Eurykleia," Telemakhos says.

She goes. She pulls the door shut and locks it behind her.

Telemakhos lies on his back. The harper's music comes up faintly through the stone floors — the suitors are still down there, will be down there tomorrow, and the day after. But the day after tomorrow, or soon after that, there will be a ship at the harbor waiting. He has never sailed anywhere. He has never spoken at an assembly. He has never looked for anything.

He does not sleep for a long time.

Outside, the fires of the suitors go out one by one.
