"Here is what you need to do," Mentes says.

Tomorrow: call an assembly. Every free man in Ithaka, every household head, everyone who can walk to the meeting-place. Stand up in front of them and tell the suitors formally, publicly, with witnesses, to leave. The gods are watching. Tell them: Odysseus may be alive. He may be on his way home. Get out of his house. Do it in the open, in daylight, so everyone on the island hears it.

Then: get a ship. Twenty oarsmen. A good, fast hull. Sail first to Pylos — there is an old king there named Nestor, who fought beside your father at Troy for ten years and knew him better than anyone. Ask him: do you have news of Odysseus? Where was he last? Which way did he go? Old men remember everything. Then sail to Sparta — King Menelaos, who was one of the last Greeks to reach home and who heard things on the way that others did not. Ask him the same question.

"If they tell you he is dead," Mentes says, "come home. Grieve properly. Build him a monument so that people know he existed and mattered. Then make your mother choose one of these men, and let her go to his house. And then —" He stops. "Then you decide what you do about the estate. You are old enough."

"And if they tell me he's alive?"

"Then endure one more year. Trust him. He is not a man who leaves things unfinished."

The noise of the suitors fills the hall around them — laughter, a crash of something, someone calling for more wine.

Telemakhos is quiet for a moment. "I will do it," he says. He says it quietly, as if the words are for himself as much as for Mentes. But he means them, and Mentes can hear that he does.
