Mentes sets down his cup and leans forward.

"I've known your father a long time," he says. "Our families go back a generation — his father Laertes and my father Anchialos were close. I've been in this hall when it was a different kind of hall. I know what Odysseus looked like standing in it."

He glances across the room at the suitors. "It looked different."

"The last I heard, he was alive." Mentes says this simply, as fact. "Held on an island by a goddess named Kalypso, the daughter of Atlas. She wants to keep him. He has no ship to offer, and the sea around that island answers to Poseidon, who has not forgiven Odysseus for blinding his son. So he is stuck. But I will tell you this —" He pauses. "Odysseus is not a man who stays stuck. He has spent his whole life getting out of situations that would have finished anyone else. He will get out of this one."

Telemakhos absorbs this. For a moment his face is very young. Then he asks: "How can you be sure? You haven't seen him in years."

"Because I know what kind of man he is," Mentes says. "And because I am looking at you."

He studies Telemakhos the way a person studies a map they have been thinking about for a long time. "You have his hands. You have his way of going still and watching before you speak. You are his son. If he knew you were holding this hall together with nothing but patience and good manners — if he knew what you are up against —" He stops. "He would move whatever he has to move to get home."

Telemakhos looks at the floor for a moment. He doesn't say anything.

Outside, the evening is coming in off the sea.
